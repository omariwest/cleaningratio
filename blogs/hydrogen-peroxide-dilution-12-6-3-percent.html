How to Dilute 12%, 6%, and 3% Hydrogen Peroxide

Use the concentration formula to dilute hydrogen peroxide accurately: 12% to 6% or 3%, 6% to 3%, and 3% to 1%, with exact amounts for common bottle sizes.

13–15 min read•Hydrogen peroxide•Dilution math

Part of the main guide

This article belongs to the Surface Cleaning Guide. For a calculator instead of manual math, use the Hydrogen Peroxide Dilution Calculator.

Quick answer

The core formula is C1 × V1 = C2 × V2: starting concentration × starting volume = target concentration × final volume.

  • 12% → 6%: equal parts 12% peroxide and water.
  • 12% → 3%: 1 part 12% peroxide + 3 parts water.
  • 6% → 3%: equal parts 6% peroxide and water.
  • 3% → 1%: 1 part 3% peroxide + 2 parts water.

For a 1 liter final mix, that means 500 mL of 12% + 500 mL water for 6%; 250 mL of 12% + 750 mL water for 3%; 500 mL of 6% + 500 mL water for 3%; or about 333 mL of 3% + 667 mL water for 1%.

Safety: 12% hydrogen peroxide is not the same handling category as household 3%. ATSDR states that concentrations above 10% are corrosive to skin, eyes, and mucous membranes. This page gives dilution math, not permission to use the resulting concentration on every surface or for every disinfecting job. See ATSDR hydrogen peroxide guidance.

The formula behind every peroxide dilution

Use C1V1 = C2V2. C1 is the concentration you have, V1 is the amount of that starting solution you need, C2 is the concentration you want, and V2 is the final total volume.

Rearranged for the amount of starting peroxide:

V1 = (C2 × V2) ÷ C1

Then calculate water as final volume − starting peroxide volume. The percentages can be entered as 12, 6, 3, and 1 because the same percent units cancel on both sides.

For example, to make 500 mL of 3% from 12%: (3 × 500) ÷ 12 = 125 mL of 12% peroxide. Add water until the final volume is 500 mL, which means 375 mL water.

Choose the target concentration before you measure

The easiest way to make a bad peroxide dilution is to start pouring before deciding the exact final concentration and final volume. Write both numbers down first. “Make this weaker” is not enough information; “make 500 mL at 3% from a 12% bottle” is.

The target concentration should come from the product directions, the surface instructions, or a procedure that actually specifies that strength. Do not choose 6%, 3%, or 1% only because it appears in a chart. A mathematically correct mixture can still be wrong for the material, cleaning task, or disinfecting claim.

This also prevents a common volume error: if the goal is 500 mL final, you do not take 500 mL of concentrate and then add water. The calculated concentrate amount is only one portion of that final 500 mL.

12% hydrogen peroxide to 6%

Halving 12% to 6% is the simplest case because the target concentration is exactly half the starting concentration. Half of the final mixture is 12% peroxide and half is water.

  • 500 mL final: 250 mL of 12% + 250 mL water.
  • 1 L final: 500 mL of 12% + 500 mL water.
  • 32 oz final: 16 oz of 12% + 16 oz water.

Do not confuse “equal parts” with pouring water into the original bottle until it looks doubled. Measure the amount you need into a compatible container and make the final volume deliberately.

12% hydrogen peroxide to 3%

Going from 12% to 3% reduces the concentration to one quarter. The final mixture is one quarter 12% peroxide and three quarters water.

  • 500 mL final: 125 mL of 12% + 375 mL water.
  • 1 L final: 250 mL of 12% + 750 mL water.
  • 32 oz final: 8 oz of 12% + 24 oz water.

Household peroxide is commonly sold at 3%, but a diluted 12% product should not be assumed to become identical to every store-bought 3% product. Labels, stabilizers, packaging, and intended uses can differ.

6% hydrogen peroxide to 3%

Six percent to 3% is another half-strength dilution: equal parts 6% peroxide and water.

  • 250 mL final: 125 mL of 6% + 125 mL water.
  • 500 mL final: 250 mL of 6% + 250 mL water.
  • 1 L final: 500 mL of 6% + 500 mL water.

If the 6% product was made for hair, laboratory, industrial, or another specialty use, do not assume its label allows the same household-surface uses as a 3% drugstore bottle.

3% hydrogen peroxide to 1%

Three percent to 1% uses one part 3% peroxide to two parts water. This is the same math already used in our broader Hydrogen Peroxide Dilution Chart for Cleaning.

  • 500 mL final: about 167 mL of 3% + 333 mL water.
  • 1 L final: about 333 mL of 3% + 667 mL water.
  • 32 oz final: about 10.7 oz of 3% + 21.3 oz water.

Lower concentration does not automatically mean “safe on every floor” or “still disinfects exactly the same.” Use the product and surface instructions for the job you are actually doing.

Dilution math is not the same as a disinfecting claim

The CDC describes commercially available 3% hydrogen peroxide as an effective disinfectant on inanimate surfaces in specific contexts, but disinfecting performance depends on concentration, product formulation, surface, and contact time. Diluting a product changes those conditions.

If the goal is disinfection rather than ordinary cleaning, use a product with directions for that use and follow its required wet-contact time. Do not assume a 1% solution made from 3% keeps every claim printed on the original bottle.

Reference: CDC Chemical Disinfectants.

Measure and store peroxide carefully

  • Use the concentration on the actual label. Do not calculate from a remembered percentage.
  • Measure volume, not capfuls. A marked cylinder or dedicated measuring cup is more reliable.
  • Avoid skin and eye exposure. Higher concentrations can cause more severe injury; NIOSH lists the eyes, skin, and respiratory system among target organs.
  • Keep the original label information with the mixture. Do not leave an unlabeled clear liquid where it could be mistaken for water.
  • Protect peroxide from light and contamination. Hydrogen peroxide decomposes to water and oxygen and is normally sold in light-protective packaging.

For higher concentrations, follow the supplier's safety data and handling instructions. If the purpose is ordinary household cleaning and you do not need 12% for another reason, starting with a lower-concentration product is simpler.

Why milliliters are better than rough kitchen measures

Percentage dilution works best when the same volume unit is used throughout the calculation. Milliliters make that easy: 125 mL, 250 mL, and 333 mL can be read directly from a graduated measure without converting tablespoons, cups, and fluid ounces back and forth.

For larger 32-ounce examples, fluid ounces are shown because that is a common U.S. bottle size. But if the calculated amount is awkward, convert both parts to milliliters and measure there. The concentration does not care which volume unit you choose as long as the units stay consistent.

Common dilution mistakes

  • Using “parts” when the target is a final concentration. For percentage-to-percentage dilution, use C1V1 = C2V2.
  • Adding the calculated water to a full bottle. The table gives a final volume, not an amount to add to an already full container.
  • Ignoring the starting percentage. A 6% bottle needs different math from a 12% bottle.
  • Assuming a diluted specialty product becomes a household product. Formulation and labeling still matter.
  • Mixing peroxide with other cleaners. Keep peroxide separate unless the product instructions explicitly state compatibility.
