70% vs 91% Isopropyl Alcohol for Cleaning

Both strengths can be useful, but 91% is not automatically the better cleaner because the number is higher. The better choice depends on the surface, the job, and whether you need working time or faster evaporation.

Quick answer

For ordinary small-surface cleaning where the material allows alcohol, 70% isopropyl alcohol is usually the simpler all-around choice. It has more water and gives more working time before evaporating. 91% dries faster and leaves less water behind, which can suit some technical cleaning jobs when the equipment or surface manufacturer allows it.

For disinfection, do not choose by percentage alone. Use the product directions for the surface and claim you need, and keep the surface wet for the required contact time.

CDC guidance says alcohol's bactericidal activity is strongest in roughly 60%–90% solutions in water and notes that rapid evaporation can make long contact times difficult. See CDC Chemical Disinfectants.

The useful comparison is not “70 is stronger” versus “91 is stronger.” Concentration changes both antimicrobial behavior and evaporation.

Why 70% is often preferred for routine cleaning

Alcohol works partly by denaturing proteins. CDC guidance explains that water helps this process, which is one reason very high alcohol concentration is not automatically more bactericidal.

Seventy percent also remains wet longer than 91%. That can be useful on a small hard surface because a product cannot complete a required wet-contact time after it has already evaporated.

This does not make 70% universal. Some plastics, coatings, rubber, lenses, painted finishes, and specialty surfaces can be damaged by repeated alcohol exposure.

When 91% can make more sense

Ninety-one percent contains much less water and normally evaporates faster. That can help where fast drying and lower water exposure matter more than long wet contact.

Some electronics-service, adhesive-preparation, resin, or precision-cleaning workflows specify a higher alcohol concentration. Follow the device, component, coating, or chemical manufacturer's instructions rather than assuming every electronic or plastic surface can take 91% IPA.

Fast drying is useful only when it matches the job. It is not a reason to replace 70% for routine wiping, and it is not permission to use 91% on a screen, coating, seal, or adhesive whose manufacturer restricts alcohol.

Cleaning and disinfecting are different jobs

Cleaning removes dirt, oil, dust, and residue. Disinfecting is tied to specific product directions, surfaces, organisms, and contact times. A bottle labeled “91% isopropyl alcohol” is not automatically a better disinfectant than a properly used 70% product.

The CDC notes that alcohol is most practical on smaller surfaces because it evaporates rapidly. For a labeled disinfecting job, pre-clean visible soil when required and apply enough product to maintain the stated wet-contact time.

Surfaces that need extra care

CDC guidance notes that repeated alcohol exposure can discolor, swell, harden, or crack some rubber and plastics and can damage some optical materials.

  • Screens and coated glass: follow the device maker's cleaning instructions.
  • Painted, varnished, acrylic, or specialty plastic surfaces: do not assume alcohol compatibility.
  • Rubber, seals, and adhesives: repeated solvent exposure can change some materials.

If the manufacturer recommends a milder cleaner, use that rather than moving from 70% to 91%.

Can you turn 91% isopropyl alcohol into 70%?

Yes. To make 100 mL of 70% from 91%, use about 76.9 mL of 91% isopropyl alcohol and add water until the total volume reaches 100 mL.

For 16 oz, 24 oz, 32 oz, 500 mL, and 1 L quantities, use the dedicated 91% to 70% dilution chart. If you may start from either 91% or 99%, use How to Make 70% Isopropyl Alcohol from 91% or 99%.

Do not mix isopropyl alcohol with bleach

Keep alcohol separate from bleach and other household cleaners. Mixing bleach with rubbing alcohol can create hazardous byproducts. See Can You Mix Bleach and Rubbing Alcohol?.

Handle both strengths as flammable solvents

Both 70% and 91% isopropyl alcohol are highly flammable. Keep them away from flames, cigarettes, sparks, hot appliances, and other ignition sources, and avoid using large amounts in a poorly ventilated enclosed area.

Store the product in its original labeled container, tightly closed and away from heat. Faster evaporation does not make 91% safer to handle.

Common questions

Is 70% or 91% better for cleaning?

For routine small-surface cleaning where alcohol is compatible, 70% is usually the more practical default. Use 91% when a specific job benefits from faster drying and the surface manufacturer permits it.

Is 91% better because it is stronger?

No. Higher concentration is not automatically better antimicrobial performance. Water contributes to alcohol's action, while faster evaporation can reduce wet-contact time.

Can I use 91% on electronics?

Only when the device or component manufacturer allows it. Electronics differ in coatings, adhesives, plastics, seals, and screen treatments.

Can 70% disinfect a surface?

Use the product according to its label. If you need a specific disinfecting claim, follow the approved surface directions and contact time rather than relying on percentage alone.
