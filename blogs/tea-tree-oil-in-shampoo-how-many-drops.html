How Much Tea Tree Oil to Add to Shampoo?

A practical drops chart for people who want to add tea tree oil to shampoo without turning a finished shampoo bottle into an unnecessarily strong scalp mixture.

12–14 min read•Tea tree oil•Shampoo dilution

Part of the main guide

This article belongs to the Essential Oil Dilution Guide. For generic tea tree oil dilution rather than shampoo, use the Tea Tree Oil Dilution Ratio Chart.

Quick answer

If you are adding tea tree oil to a plain rinse-off shampoo for personal use, keep the amount small. CleaningRatio uses 0.5% to 1% as a conservative home-blending range, not as a medical treatment dose. Using the same practical estimate of about 20 drops per mL used in our other essential-oil charts:

  • 100 mL shampoo: about 10 drops at 0.5% or 20 drops at 1%.
  • 250 mL shampoo: about 25 drops at 0.5% or 50 drops at 1%.
  • 300 mL shampoo: about 30 drops at 0.5% or 60 drops at 1%.
  • 12 fl oz / 355 mL: about 36 drops at 0.5% or 71 drops at 1%.
  • 16 fl oz / 473 mL: about 47 drops at 0.5% or 95 drops at 1%.

A commercial shampoo is already a finished formulation, so the cleaner option is a shampoo that was formulated with tea tree oil from the start. If you modify shampoo yourself, mix a small batch first instead of changing a whole new bottle.

Important: tea tree oil should not be swallowed and can cause redness or irritation in some people. Keep it away from the eyes and stop using the mixture if the scalp becomes irritated. See the NCCIH tea tree oil safety summary.

Tea tree oil drops chart for shampoo bottles

The chart is simple percentage math. At 0.5%, tea tree oil is about 0.5 mL per 100 mL of finished mixture. At 1%, it is about 1 mL per 100 mL. The drop counts are estimates because droppers do not produce identical drops.

If you only want to test whether your scalp tolerates the blend, the 30 mL or 60 mL row is more useful than modifying a 12- or 16-ounce bottle. It limits waste and makes it easier to stop if the mixture is too strong.

Why adding essential oil to a full shampoo bottle is not ideal

Shampoo is not just soap and water. It is a finished mixture of surfactants, water, fragrance, thickeners, preservatives, and other ingredients designed to stay stable at a particular formula. Adding a concentrated essential oil changes that formula.

Tea tree oil also does not dissolve into water on its own. Shampoo surfactants can help disperse a small addition, but a home bottle is not the same as a product developed and stability-tested with tea tree oil already built into it. Poor mixing can leave one use weaker and another use stronger.

That is why this page does not tell you to keep pouring until the shampoo “smells strong.” Scent is a bad measuring method. Measure the amount, mix thoroughly, and use a small batch first.

What about the 5% tea tree oil shampoo study?

A randomized trial published in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology studied a 5% tea tree oil shampoo in 126 people with mild to moderate dandruff. The tea-tree group improved more than the placebo group over four weeks. That study is useful evidence that a formulated tea tree shampoo can have an effect, but it is not a recipe telling you to pour 5% pure tea tree oil into any commercial shampoo.

The study used a prepared shampoo product with a defined concentration. A home addition can differ in mixing, ingredients, preservation, exposure near the eyes, and how evenly the oil is distributed. If your goal is dandruff treatment rather than simply adding a small amount of tea tree oil to a rinse-off product, a properly formulated product is the more controlled choice.

Study reference: Satchell et al., 5% tea tree oil shampoo trial.

A better way to test a DIY shampoo blend

  1. Start with a small amount of shampoo. Use 30 mL or 60 mL rather than the whole bottle.
  2. Measure the tea tree oil. For 30 mL, about 3 drops gives roughly 0.5% and 6 drops gives roughly 1% using the 20-drops-per-mL estimate.
  3. Mix thoroughly. Do not leave visible oil sitting on the surface of the shampoo.
  4. Keep it away from the eyes. Tea tree oil and shampoo can both irritate the eye area.
  5. Rinse normally. This page is about a rinse-off shampoo mixture, not a leave-on scalp oil.
  6. Stop if irritation appears. Burning, persistent itching, redness, or swelling is a reason to wash it off and discontinue the mixture.

For a leave-on or pre-wash scalp oil, do not reuse this shampoo chart. Use the Essential Oil Dilution for Hair Oil and Scalp Use guide because the carrier and exposure time are different.

How many drops for common shampoo sizes?

How many drops in 8 oz of shampoo?

Eight fluid ounces is about 237 mL. At 0.5%, that is about 24 drops. At 1%, it is about 47 drops. If this is your first attempt, a smaller test batch is easier to evaluate than altering the entire 8-ounce bottle.

How many drops in 12 oz of shampoo?

Twelve fluid ounces is about 355 mL. The chart gives about 36 drops at 0.5% or 71 drops at 1%.

How many drops in 16 oz of shampoo?

Sixteen fluid ounces is about 473 mL. The chart gives about 47 drops at 0.5% or 95 drops at 1%.

Those numbers can look large because the bottle itself is large. They are still estimates, not a reason to count dozens of drops into a bottle by eye. For larger quantities, measuring in milliliters is more consistent than counting individual drops.

Tea tree oil, dandruff, and an itchy scalp

Tea tree oil is often searched together with dandruff, but an itchy or flaky scalp does not always have one cause. Product irritation, seborrheic dermatitis, psoriasis, eczema, fungal conditions, and other problems can look similar from the user's point of view.

The 5% shampoo trial is encouraging, but it does not prove that every DIY tea tree mixture will treat dandruff, and NCCIH notes that evidence for many tea tree oil uses remains limited. If scalp symptoms are persistent, painful, spreading, producing sores, or not improving, use appropriate medical advice rather than making the mixture stronger.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Do not use undiluted tea tree oil as shampoo. Pure essential oil is not a substitute for a rinse-off cleanser.
  • Do not treat “more drops” as “more effective.” Irritation risk can rise while the formula becomes less predictable.
  • Do not copy a 5% clinical shampoo concentration into a random commercial bottle. A formulated study product and a home modification are not equivalent.
  • Do not swallow tea tree oil. NCCIH warns that oral ingestion can cause serious symptoms.
  • Do not use the shampoo chart for a leave-on scalp oil. Use a carrier-oil dilution chart instead.
  • Do not keep using an oxidized old bottle of essential oil. Heat, light, and air exposure can increase the chance of skin reactions.

Common questions

Can I put tea tree oil directly into shampoo?

You can physically mix a small amount into some shampoos, but the shampoo was not necessarily formulated or stability-tested for that addition. A pre-formulated tea tree shampoo is the more controlled option. If you experiment, use a small batch first and keep the concentration conservative.

Is 20 drops per 100 mL too much?

Using the chart's estimate of 20 drops per mL, 20 drops in 100 mL is about 1%. Drop size varies, so milliliters are better for larger batches.

Should I add tea tree oil to medicated shampoo?

Do not automatically modify a medicated shampoo. Its active ingredients and directions are already set by the product. Use it as labeled unless a clinician or the manufacturer tells you otherwise.

Can I use this mixture every day?

There is no universal schedule for a homemade mixture. Follow the base shampoo's normal directions and pay attention to scalp tolerance. If irritation develops, stop rather than reducing the interval while continuing the same irritating formula.
