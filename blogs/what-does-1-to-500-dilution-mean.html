What Does a 1:500 Dilution Mean?

A 1:500 cleaning dilution usually means 1 part concentrate plus 500 parts water. See exact amounts for common bottles, a gallon, and cleaning buckets.

Quick answer

A 1:500 dilution usually means 1 part concentrate + 500 parts water, so the finished mix has 501 total parts.

  • 500 mL final: about 1.00 mL concentrate + 499.00 mL water.
  • 1 L final: about 2.00 mL concentrate + 998.00 mL water.
  • 32 oz final: about 0.064 fl oz / 1.89 mL concentrate.
  • 1 gallon final: about 0.26 fl oz / 7.56 mL concentrate.

Check the label wording first. Some manufacturers use a ratio to describe concentrate-to-total-solution instead of concentrate-to-water. If the label gives mL per liter or oz per gallon, use that direct dose.

What 1:500 means on a cleaning label

Under the common label-style convention used across CleaningRatio, the first number is concentrate and the second is water. A 1:500 mix therefore uses one equal measure of product for every 500 equal measures of water.

This is a very dilute mixture. Small measuring errors matter more than they do at 1:10 or 1:20, especially in a spray bottle. Do not free-pour a concentrate when the intended dose is only a few milliliters.

If the product says “1 in 500,” “1:500 final,” or gives a fixed dose, follow the manufacturer's own wording rather than forcing it into this convention.

The simple 1:500 formula

  • Concentrate = Final Volume ÷ 501
  • Water = Final Volume − Concentrate

For 1 liter, divide 1000 mL by 501. That gives about 2.00 mL concentrate; the rest is water.

For the general parts-versus-final-volume logic, use How to Calculate Dilution Ratio.

Use a precise measure for small bottles

A 16- or 32-ounce bottle at 1:500 needs only about 0.94 to 1.89 mL of concentrate. A capful or splash can be several times the intended amount.

Use a dedicated marked syringe, medicine-style cup, or graduated cleaning measure. Never use the same tool later for food or medicine.

A weak final dilution does not mean the undiluted concentrate is harmless. Follow its protective-equipment and handling instructions while measuring.

Do not strengthen the mix because the amount looks small. The point of a 1:500 label is that the concentrate is designed to be used at a low dose for that stated task.

How strong is a 1:500 mix?

Under the 1 part concentrate + 500 parts water convention, concentrate makes up about 0.200% of the finished mixture. That number describes how much of the concentrate is present; it does not tell you the percentage of any active ingredient inside the concentrate.

For comparison, 1:200 is about 0.5% concentrate by finished volume. A 1:500 mix is therefore weaker again than 1:400 and about two-fifths as concentrated as 1:200.

See What Does a 1:200 Dilution Mean? and What Does a 1:400 Dilution Mean?.

How to mix 1:500 reliably

  1. Confirm the label convention. Make sure the ratio really means concentrate to water.
  2. Choose the final volume. Do not calculate from a vague “half bottle.”
  3. Measure concentrate in milliliters. This matters at high dilution ratios.
  4. Add water as directed. Follow the label's order of mixing when specified.
  5. Label the secondary container. Include product name and dilution.

For odd sizes, use the Cleaning Dilution Calculator.

Common 1:500 mistakes

  • Dividing by 500 instead of 501. Under this convention the finished mix has 501 total parts.
  • Free-pouring the concentrate. Tiny doses need a marked measure.
  • Ignoring a clearer manufacturer dose. mL/L or oz/gal directions beat a generic ratio assumption.
  • Making the mix stronger for heavy soil without label support. That changes the intended dilution.
  • Using the same dilution for every task. One product can specify different ratios for different jobs.
